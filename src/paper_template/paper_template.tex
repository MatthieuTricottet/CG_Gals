  \abstract{
    We ask whether galaxies in compact groups (CGs) remain special when they are compared with regular-group analogues rather than with the field alone. We use the CG$_4$ sample inherited from the Tricottet et al. construction after removing split systems, leaving <<CG4_Groups_nonsplit_N>> compact groups, and compare it with three regular-group controls: RG$_4$, Control$_{4B}$, and Control$_{4C}$. These controls respectively test true four-member regular groups, the luminous cores of richer regular groups, and projected compact cores around the brightest group galaxy. We combine Galaxy Zoo vote-fraction morphologies, SDSS stellar masses and specific star-formation rates, optical colours, emission-line and AGN-like diagnostics, and both adjusted and one-to-one matched control comparisons. CG$_4$ galaxies are more often classified as elliptical/smooth and less often as spiral/features than galaxies in the controls. This morphology signal survives adjustment for stellar mass, redshift, rank, and available group-scale quantities, and is also present in matched comparisons. The star-formation signal is weaker: CG$_4$ satellites have a lower raw star-forming fraction, especially relative to RG$_4$, but this contrast is not comparably robust after the adjusted and matched analyses. Among galaxies that remain star-forming, we find no robust compact-group offset from the star-forming main sequence. Colours, AGN-like fractions, magnitude gaps, and the limited H\(\alpha\) diagnostics do not support a simple instantaneous-quenching, fossil-group, or AGN-driven explanation. A projected pairwise tidal-index diagnostic suggests that local tidal density accounts for part of the morphology signal. Compact groups therefore host a more morphologically evolved population, consistent with accumulated transformation in very dense group cores and/or preprocessing rather than a uniquely isolated compact-group quenching mode.
   }

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Compact groups of galaxies are useful laboratories for environmental evolution because they combine high projected galaxy densities with relatively low velocity dispersions \citep{Hickson82,Hickson+92}. In such configurations, repeated tidal encounters, dynamical friction, mergers, gas stripping or consumption, and disk heating can plausibly accelerate the transformation of galaxies compared with more diffuse environments \citep{Barnes85,Barnes89,Verdes-Montenegro+01}. This expectation is closely connected to the broader morphology-density and star-formation-environment relation, in which galaxy structure and star-formation activity vary systematically with local density \citep{Dressler80}.

The interpretation of compact groups is nevertheless ambiguous. Projected selection can include chance alignments, and many Hickson-like systems need not be isolated bound entities. Simulations and redshift-space analyses have long shown that apparent compact groups may be transient alignments, dense substructures, or cores embedded in looser groups and richer haloes \citep{Mamon86,DiazGimenez&Mamon10,DiazGimenez+12,Zheng&Shen21}. The relevant observational question is therefore not only whether compact-group galaxies differ from field galaxies, but whether they remain different from galaxies occupying regular-group analogues selected with comparable luminosity, redshift, and membership constraints.

Recent structural work strengthens this motivation. Using S-PLUS imaging and single-S\'ersic measurements, \citet{Montaguth2023,Montaguth2025,Montaguth2026NonIsolated,Montaguth2026MassSize} identified transition galaxies and trends in the \(R_{\rm e}\)--S\'ersic-index plane that point to structural transformation in compact groups, with stronger signatures in non-isolated compact groups and host structures. These studies suggest that compact groups can mark sites of cumulative structural processing. They do not, however, by themselves answer whether compact-group galaxies remain distinct after comparison with regular-group cores selected from the same parent catalogues.

Our previous sample-construction paper \citep{Tricottet+25} showed that many compact groups are not isolated systems, but appear as dense cores or substructures of richer environments. This result motivates the stricter comparison made here. We use the same compact-group and regular-group sample framework to ask whether CG galaxies are still special after controlling for stellar mass, redshift, galaxy rank, and available group-scale properties. We then ask which observable carries the signal: star-formation class, Galaxy Zoo morphology, optical colour, AGN-like activity, fossil-like magnitude gaps, projected phase-space position, or local projected tidal density.

The paper is organized as follows. Section~2 describes the samples, the sSFR and morphology classifications, and the statistical hierarchy. Sections~3 and 4 present baseline population differences and group-scale diagnostics. Section~5 tests which signals survive adjusted and matched comparisons and examines secondary diagnostics. Section~6 relates the results to recent structural studies of compact-group galaxies, and Section~7 summarizes the conclusions.

\section{Data and classifications}
\subsection{Samples}
We use the compact-group and control samples constructed in our previous study \cite{Tricottet+25}.
We briefly recall their construction here. Galaxy properties are taken from the SDSS DR12 catalogue
of \citet{Tempel+17}, while regular groups are drawn from the SDSS DR7 group catalogue of \citet{Lim+17}; the two
catalogues were matched through SDSS object identifiers to define the Lim--Tempel working catalogue. Compact groups
are selected from the modified Hickson compact-group catalogue of \citet{DiazGimenez+18}. Starting from the reliable
HMCGs with exactly four members, we retain only systems in which all galaxies are present in the Lim--Tempel catalogue and
impose the volume- and luminosity-complete limits $0.005 < z_{\rm BGG} < 0.0452$ and $M_{r,\rm BGG}\leq -21.81$, with
all 4 galaxies lying within 3 magnitudes of the brightest group galaxy. This defines the parent \CG{} sample of
78 compact groups. The regular-group comparison samples are built from Lim--Tempel groups satisfying the same redshift
and BGG-luminosity limits, with at least 4 members and at least 3 satellites within 3 magnitudes of the BGG.
From this parent control catalogue, we define \RG{} groups as regular groups with exactly four members, excluding
systems identical to a \CG{}, and we additionally use the \CB{} and \CC{} samples, formed respectively from
the 4 brightest galaxies and from the BGG plus its 3 closest projected companions in each parent regular group,
after removing groups that share galaxies with \CG{}s.
In contrast to our previous study, we remove CGs classified as \emph{split} following \cite{Zheng&Shen21}.
This reduces the compact-group sample from <<CG4_Groups_withsplit_N>> to
<<CG4_Groups_nonsplit_N>> groups. We also remove the systems associated with Lim group 3688 from our \CB{} and \CC{} samples. This group contains 6 galaxies
with redshifts between 0.032 and 0.034, and 1 with a redshift of 0.048. This group appears to be spurious, and the
outlying galaxy, although not the BGG, is selected in both our original \CB{} and \CC{} samples, significantly affecting the group velocity
dispersion ($1979$ and $1981\, \kms$, respectively).

The three regular-group controls serve different physical purposes. RG$_4$ contains true regular groups with exactly four selected members and is the most direct four-member regular-group comparison. Control$_{4B}$ contains the four brightest galaxies in richer regular groups and tests whether the CG$_4$ population resembles the luminous core of an ordinary group. Control$_{4C}$ contains the BGG plus the three closest projected companions and is the most compact-core-like ordinary-group analogue. CG$_4$ is therefore tested against both equal-membership groups and regular-group cores, rather than against a single control definition.

We use SDSS DR <<DATA_RELEASE>> to retrieve stellar masses, star formation rates (SFRs), and Galaxy Zoo morphologies
\citep{GalaxyZoo1}. We specifically extract the columns \texttt{sfr\_tot\_p50},
\texttt{specsfr\_tot\_p50}, and \texttt{lgm\_tot\_p50} from the \texttt{galSpecExtra} table, and
\texttt{p\_el\_debiased} and \texttt{p\_cs\_debiased} from the \texttt{zooSpec} table.

We identify BPT-classifiable galaxies by requiring measured values of both emission-line ratios
\[
\log_{10}\!\bigl([\mathrm{N\,II}]\lambda6584/\mathrm{H}\alpha\bigr)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\log_{10}\!\bigl([\mathrm{O\,III}]\lambda5007/\mathrm{H}\beta\bigr).
\]
Among these galaxies, we flag broad AGN-like systems if they satisfy either
\[
\log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{N\,II}]}{\mathrm{H}\alpha} > 0,
\]
or if it lies above the empirical demarcation of \cite{Kauffmann+03a}:
\[
\log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{O\,III}]}{\mathrm{H}\beta}
> \frac{0.61}{\log_{10}([\mathrm{N\,II}]/\mathrm{H}\alpha)-0.05} \;+\; 1.3,
\]
in which case they are excluded from the GMM calibration sample. Galaxies with missing diagnostic line ratios are BPT-unclassified, not proven non-AGN. They are retained for the broad sSFR status assignment when valid SDSS stellar mass and sSFR estimates are available, while the emission-line/AGN-like analysis below treats BPT-unclassified objects separately. The BPT-classifiable subset is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:BPT}.

Galaxies with \texttt{specsfr\_tot\_p50} equal to -9999 in the SDSS are classified as quenched
(for display purposes, they are assigned the arbitrary low value $10^{<<sSFR_QUENCHED>>}$ Gyr\(^{-1}\)).
The remaining galaxies are separated into
star-forming and passive populations using a two‐component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) in the \(\log M_*\)–\(\log\mathrm{sSFR}\) plane.
Its parameters are determined by minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the empirical and model densities
(\cite{Kullback1951,McLachlanPeel2000}). For any trial parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), we reconstruct the mixture means
\(\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_i\}\), covariances \(\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_i\}\), and weights \(\{w_i\}\) via a Cholesky‐like decomposition and
logistic mapping, optionally constraining the second component’s mean to isolate the passive population (\cite{Dempster1977}).
The KL divergence is estimated by constructing a 2D histogram of the data and summing \(p_\mathrm{data}\ln(p_\mathrm{data}/p_\mathrm{GMM})\),
with a small regularization \(\epsilon\) to avoid singularities (\cite{Tojeiro2009,Bisigello2018}). We optimize \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\)
via L-BFGS-B (with fallback to Nelder–Mead) over multiple guided and random initializations, selecting the solution with lowest divergence
(\cite{Pedregosa2011,Bishop2006}). Initial means are seeded by a median split in \(\log\mathrm{sSFR}\) to improve convergence and robustness
against local minima (\cite{Rousseeuw1987}). In the final fit, one component naturally aligns with the high-\(\mathrm{sSFR}\) “star‐forming
sequence,” while the other captures the low-\(\mathrm{sSFR}\) “passive” cloud, providing a data‐driven division that agrees with previous
multi‐Gaussian decompositions of the SFR–\(M_*\) plane (\cite{Wuyts2011,Hahn2019}). The decision boundaries, defined as the
loci where the posterior probabilities of adjacent components are equal, provide an objective criterion for
delineating star‐forming from passive galaxies. The GMM calibration excludes galaxies flagged as AGN-like where the required BPT ratios are available, while line-missing galaxies can still receive a broad sSFR class from their SDSS mass and sSFR measurements.

\subsection{Morphology classification}
Morphologies are determined via the Galaxy Zoo citizen‐science decision tree, in which each galaxy image receives multiple
independent volunteer classifications that are aggregated into debiased vote fractions \citep{GalaxyZoo1}. Those fractions are provided in the
\texttt{zooSpec} table of the SDSS database through the \texttt{p\_el\_debiased} and \texttt{p\_cs\_debiased} columns. We classify a galaxy as
``elliptical/smooth'' if \texttt{p\_el\_debiased} is greater than 0.5, and as ``spiral/features'' if \texttt{p\_cs\_debiased} is greater than 0.5.
All other cases are classified as ``uncertain''. These are vote-fraction classes, not a full structural decomposition. In particular, the catalogue used here does not provide a clean E/S0 separation, so references below to elliptical or early-type galaxies should be read as Galaxy Zoo smooth/early-type proxies rather than as a bulge-disc structural classification.

\subsection{Statistical strategy}

The analyses are organized hierarchically. Raw tables and exact tests provide descriptive baseline comparisons. Fisher or Barnard exact tests are used for two-sample categorical comparisons depending on the table structure, while rank-sum or bootstrap comparisons are used for distributional and median differences. Spearman and Pearson correlations are used only as diagnostics of monotonic or linear trends; when the coefficients are small, we interpret them as weak even if the p-values are formally small. Holm or Benjamini--Hochberg corrections are applied where multiple related tests are performed. The primary robustness layer consists of mass-, rank-, and environment-adjusted binomial models with group-clustered standard errors, followed by one-to-one matched control comparisons. Colours, AGN-like fractions, H\(\alpha\) equivalent widths, magnitude gaps, crossing-time trends, projected phase space, and the pairwise tidal index are secondary diagnostics. The main conclusions below rely on signals that survive the adjusted and matched analyses, not on isolated exploratory tests.

\section{Relation to recent structural studies of compact-group galaxies}

A complementary interpretation of the present results is provided by the recent series of studies by
\citet{Montaguth2023,Montaguth2025,Montaguth2026NonIsolated,Montaguth2026MassSize}.
Using multiwavelength S-PLUS imaging and single-S\'ersic structural fits, these authors identified a population of
``transition'' galaxies, broadly corresponding to red systems with relatively low or intermediate S\'ersic index, whose
location in the $R_{\rm e}$--$n$ plane suggests ongoing structural transformation in compact groups. In particular,
\citet{Montaguth2023} found that compact-group transition galaxies include a population that is smaller and more
centrally concentrated than analogous field galaxies, while \citet{Montaguth2025} showed that this signature is
stronger in non-isolated compact groups embedded in larger structures. \citet{Montaguth2026NonIsolated} further
compared non-isolated compact groups with their surrounding host-group galaxies and found that the compact-group
members display higher quenched fractions, a larger early-type fraction, and distinct transition-galaxy
($R_{\rm e}$--$n$) distributions. Finally, \citet{Montaguth2026MassSize} showed that the environmental dependence of the
stellar mass–size relation is weak for early- and late-type galaxies, but strongest for transition galaxies, whose
mass–size slopes and S\'ersic-index distributions become more early-type-like in groups and compact groups.

Our analysis addresses a related but stricter question. Rather than comparing compact groups primarily with the
field, or non-isolated compact groups with their immediate host structures, we ask whether compact-group galaxies
remain different from galaxies in regular four-member group analogues after controlling for stellar mass, redshift,
rank, and available group-scale quantities. In this framework, the most robust residual signal is morphological:
compact-group membership remains associated with an enhanced probability of being classified as elliptical and a
reduced probability of being classified as spiral. By contrast, the passive and star-forming satellite fractions are
more sensitive to the adopted control sample and to the adjustment or matching procedure.

The two sets of results are therefore not contradictory. The structural work of
\citet{Montaguth2023,Montaguth2025,Montaguth2026NonIsolated,Montaguth2026MassSize} identifies the likely
nature of the transformation: disk fading, increasing central concentration, and/or bulge growth in galaxies occupying
an intermediate region between blue late-type disks and red early-type systems. Our results indicate that, when
compact groups are compared with regular-group analogues rather than with the field alone, the clearest surviving
signature is the accumulated morphological state of the galaxy population, whereas an instantaneous star-formation
offset is less robust. This suggests that compact groups should not be interpreted simply as environments with
ongoing enhanced quenching at the present epoch. They are more plausibly very dense regions in which past
interactions, local tidal density, and preprocessing in larger haloes have already shifted part of the galaxy population
toward earlier morphologies.

A useful way to reconcile these views is to regard the compact-group signal as primarily structural and cumulative.
In that picture, the compact configuration may accelerate morphological transformation, but some of the observed
differences may also reflect the fact that many compact groups are the dense cores or substructures of larger systems.
Future work combining the structural diagnostics of S-PLUS-like analyses, especially $R_{\rm e}$, S\'ersic index,
transition-galaxy classifications, and mass–size residuals, with the matched regular-group control framework used
here would provide a direct test of whether the compact-group transformation is unique to compact configurations
or is the high-density limit of more general group preprocessing.

\section{Conclusion}

We have tested whether galaxies in CG$_4$ compact groups remain special after removing split systems and comparing them with three regular-group analogues: RG$_4$, Control$_{4B}$, and Control$_{4C}$. The comparison was designed to separate compact configurations from ordinary four-member groups, luminous group cores, and projected compact cores around BGGs.

The main result is morphological. CG$_4$ galaxies have an enhanced elliptical/smooth Galaxy Zoo fraction and a reduced spiral/features fraction. This signal survives the mass-, redshift-, rank-, and group-adjusted binomial models and is also recovered in the one-to-one matched-control analysis.

The star-formation result is weaker. CG$_4$ satellites have a lower raw star-forming fraction, especially relative to RG$_4$, but the adjusted and matched evidence is weaker or borderline. Among galaxies that remain star-forming, CG$_4$ galaxies do not show a robust offset from the fitted star-forming main sequence.

The secondary diagnostics do not identify a simple alternative driver. The colour results are control-sensitive and based on a biased colour-matched subset. AGN-like fractions and magnitude gaps do not explain the morphology signal, the H\(\alpha\) diagnostic is limited by missing age-sensitive spectral indices, and projected phase-space trends do not localize the passive difference uniquely to the nearest satellites.

We therefore interpret CG$_4$ galaxies as a more morphologically evolved population, consistent with accumulated transformation in very dense environments, dense group cores, and/or preprocessing. The pairwise tidal-index experiment suggests that local projected tidal density accounts for part of the compact-group morphology coefficient, so the result should not be read as evidence for a uniquely isolated compact-group quenching mechanism.

Future work should connect this regular-group control framework directly to structural measurements, including \(R_{\rm e}\), S\'ersic index, transition-galaxy classifications, mass-size residuals, and better spectroscopic post-starburst diagnostics. Those measurements would test whether the morphology signal isolated here is the same cumulative structural transformation highlighted by recent S-PLUS studies.
