In this process, our initial compact group sample of 78 groups reduces to

We use the SDSS DR 16 to retrieve masses, star formation rates (SFRs) and morphologies of galaxies 
assessed in Galaxy Zoo (\cite{GalaxyZoo1}). We specifically extracted fields \texttt{sfr\_tot\_p50},

\texttt{p\_el\_debiased} and \texttt{p\_cs\_debiased} from the \texttt{zooSpec} table.
       

\subsection{sSFR}
To build a general star formation classification that could be uniformly applied to all our samples, we extracted
 galaxies from the SDSS DR 16  
through the query displayed in listing \ref{lst:query}. 
% The number of galaxies in each sample is shown in table \ref{tab:sample_sizes}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Query used for selecting spectral \& photometric data from the SDSS},label={lst:query}]
    
    SELECT 
        s.specObjID,
        s.z,
        p.petroMag_r - p.extinction_r as r_obs,
        p.petroMag_u - p.extinction_u as u_obs,
        p.petroMag_g - p.extinction_g as g_obs,
        p.petroMag_i - p.extinction_i as i_obs,
        p.petroMag_z - p.extinction_z as z_obs,
        p.objID,
        g.sfr_tot_p50, g.specsfr_tot_p50, g.lgm_tot_p50,
        l.h_alpha_eqw, l.h_beta_eqw, l.oiii_5007_eqw, l.nii_6584_eqw,
        l.h_alpha_flux, l.h_beta_flux, l.oiii_5007_flux, l.nii_6584_flux,
        z.p_el_debiased AS p_E,
        z.p_cs_debiased AS p_S
    FROM SpecObj AS s
        JOIN PhotoObj AS p ON s.bestObjID = p.objID
        JOIN galSpecExtra as g ON s.specObjID = g.specObjID
        JOIN galSpecLine as l ON s.specObjID = l.specObjID
        JOIN zooSpec AS z ON s.specObjID = z.specObjID
    WHERE s.z BETWEEN 0.005 AND 0.0452
        AND (p.petroMag_r - p.extinction_r <= 17.77)
        AND s.class = 'GALAXY'
        AND g.lgm_tot_p50 > -1000
    
\end{lstlisting}

We select non-AGN galaxies by first placing them on the classical BPT diagnostic of \cite{Veilleux87} and requiring measured values of both emission-line ratios
\[
\log_{10}\!\bigl([\mathrm{N\,II}]\lambda6584/\mathrm{H}\alpha\bigr)
\quad\text{and}\quad
\log_{10}\!\bigl([\mathrm{O\,III}]\lambda5007/\mathrm{H}\beta\bigr).
\]
A galaxy is flagged as an AGN if it satisfies either  
\[
\log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{N\,II}]}{\mathrm{H}\alpha} > 0,
\]  
or if it lies above the empirical demarcation of \cite{Kauffmann+03a}:  
\[
\log_{10}\frac{[\mathrm{O\,III}]}{\mathrm{H}\beta}
> \frac{0.61}{\log_{10}([\mathrm{N\,II}]/\mathrm{H}\alpha)-0.05} \;+\; 1.3,
\]
in which case it is removed from the non-AGN sample.  All remaining galaxies—including those with missing line ratios—are retained as non-AGN.
The non-AGN selection process is shown on figure \ref{fig:BPT}.

We identify the galaxies having a \texttt{specsf\_tot\_p50} of -9999 in the SDSS as quenched 
(for graphic representation purposes, we give it here an arbitrary low value of $10^{-14.0}$ Gyr\(^{-1}\)).
We then consider the other ones and sort them between the 
star-forming and passive populations using a two‐component Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) in the \(\log M_*\)–\(\log\mathrm{sSFR}\) plane.
Its parameters determined by minimizing the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the empirical and model densities 
(\cite{Kullback1951,McLachlanPeel2000}). For any trial parameter vector \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\), we reconstruct the mixture means 
\(\{\boldsymbol{\mu}_i\}\), covariances \(\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_i\}\), and weights \(\{w_i\}\) via a Cholesky‐like decomposition and 
logistic mapping, optionally constraining the second component’s mean to isolate the passive population (\cite{Dempster1977}). 
The KL divergence is estimated by constructing a 2D histogram of the data and summing \(p_\mathrm{data}\ln(p_\mathrm{data}/p_\mathrm{GMM})\), 
with a small regularization \(\epsilon\) to avoid singularities (\cite{Tojeiro2009,Bisigello2018}). We optimize \(\boldsymbol{\theta}\) 
via L-BFGS-B (with fallback to Nelder–Mead) over multiple guided and random initializations, selecting the solution with lowest divergence 
(\cite{Pedregosa2011,Bishop2006}). Initial means are seeded by a median split in \(\log\mathrm{sSFR}\) to improve convergence and robustness 
against local minima (\cite{Rousseeuw1987}). In the final fit, one component naturally aligns with the high-\(\mathrm{sSFR}\) “star‐forming 
sequence,” while the other captures the low-\(\mathrm{sSFR}\) “passive” cloud, providing a data‐driven division that agrees with previous 
multi‐Gaussian decompositions of the SFR–\(M_*\) plane (\cite{Wuyts2011,Hahn2019}). The decision boundaries, defined as the 
loci where the posterior probabilities of adjacent components are equal, provide an objective criterion for 
delineating star‐forming from passive galaxies.

\subsection{Morphology}
Morphologies are determined via the Galaxy Zoo citizen‐science decision tree, in which each galaxy image receives multiple 
independent volunteer classifications that are aggregated into debiaised vote fractions \cite{GalaxyZoo1}. Those fractions are provided in the 
\texttt{zooSpecz} table of the SDSS databasethrough the \texttt{p\_el\_debiased} and \texttt{p\_cs\_debiased} columns. We attribute to galaxies the
morphology ``elliptical'' if the \texttt{p\_el\_debiased} value is greater than 0.5, and ``spiral'' if the \texttt{p\_cs\_debiased} value is greater than 0.5. 
Other cases are considered as ``uncertain''. The morphologies found for each sample are shown in table \ref{tab:morphologies}.
Barnard two-sided exact tests were performed to compare the fraction of each morphology between \CG{} and each control sample. The p-values found are 
$1.4 \times 10^{-3}$ for ellipticals and $1.2 \times 10^{-3}$ for spirals versus \CB{} sample, 
 $3.3 \times 10^{-3}$  and $3.4 \times 10^{-2}$  versus \CC{} and  $2.2 \times 10^{-7}$  
 and $5.9 \times 10^{-7}$  versus \RG. Morphologies of \CG, wich we identified as cores of richer groups in 
 \cite{Tricottet+25}, have morphologies significantly different from ordinary groups of four members. Figure \ref{fig:galaxies_morph_sfr} displays the sSFR against masses for galaxies from the SDSS selection and for \CG{}s galaxies.

\subsection{Morphology vs sSFR}



Starforming \CG{} are composed of 58 of their galaxies classified as spiral 
($84.1$\% once galaxies having uncertain morphology removed) and 
11 ($15.9$\% once galaxies having uncertain morphology removed) elliptical.
These figures are 809 ($88.4$\%) spiral
and 106 ($11.6$\%) elliptical galaxies for \CB{}, 
851 ($84.3$\%) spiral
and 159 ($15.7$\%) elliptical galaxies for \CC{}
and 91 ($91$\%) spiral
and 9 ($9$\%) elliptical galaxies for \RG{}. 
The probability that those fractions come from 
the same distribution is estimated from the Barnard two-sided exact test as $0.29$ against \CB,
$0.97$ against \CC{} and $0.19$ against \RG. There is thus 
neither significant excess nor lack of 
starforming elliptical galaxies in \CG{} compared to all our control samples.

If we restrict the comparison to BGGs only, we find 36 Elliptical BGGs (58.1\% of the BGGs) and
17 Spiral BGGs (27.4\%) 
 in \CG{}.
  This is to be compared to 
 405 (58.0\%) Elliptical and
 195 (27.9\%) Spiral galaxies in \CB{}, 
 424 (56.5\%) Elliptical and
 216 (28.8\%)  Spiral in \CC{},
  and  21 (37.5\%) Elliptical and
  26 (46.4\%) Spiral
  in \RG{}. 
The p-values found from two-sided Fisher exact test are 
  1.00 against \CB,
  0.88 against \CC{} and 
  0.03  against \RG. 
We can therefore see that BGG morphologies are different between \CG{} and \RG{}: \CG{} BGGs are more likely 
to be elliptical than \RG{} BGGs.

\subsection{Robustness of the satellite colour result}

The SDSS-photometry subset is not representative of the full galaxy catalogues: matched galaxies are systematically less massive, more actively star-forming and more frequently satellites than unmatched galaxies. The fractions with all four colours are 44.4\% in CG4, 33.3\% in Control4B, 49.2\% in Control4C, 45.5\% in RG4.

We therefore refit the satellite colours with cluster-robust standard errors, using catalogue-qualified group identifiers. In the pooled comparison adjusted only for stellar mass and redshift, none of the four CG$_4$ coefficients is significantly negative after Holm correction. After additionally controlling for morphology and sSFR class, negative coefficients appear in $(u-r)$ ($\Delta=-0.127$ mag, $p_{\rm Holm}=0.0177$), $(u-g)$ ($\Delta=-0.101$ mag, $p_{\rm Holm}=0.0271$). These adjusted coefficients are control-sample dependent: the significant individual comparisons are Control4C in $(u-r)$, Control4C in $(u-g)$, Control4C in $(g-r)$.

Within sSFR classes, no negative CG$_4$ residual difference survives correction for the four colours. Likewise, no within-morphology residual difference survives the same correction. Thus, the data do not isolate the colour effect to the star-forming spiral population expected for a simple interaction-triggered star-formation scenario.

Figure~\ref{fig:colour_robustness_coefficients} summarizes the model and control-sample dependence. Taken together, these tests do not establish a robust global blue offset for CG$_4$ satellites; the negative full-model coefficients should instead be treated as a control-sensitive exploratory result.

\section{What drives the compact-group differences?}

The previous sections establish that CG$_4$ galaxies differ from the control catalogues in their population mix, especially in morphology and in the star-forming fraction of satellites. We now ask whether these differences survive after controlling for stellar mass, rank and available group-scale quantities, and whether they are better interpreted as compact-group-specific evolution, pre-processing in dense halo cores, or catalogue-selection effects.



\subsection{Mass-, rank- and environment-adjusted galaxy properties}

We fit binomial models to the combined galaxy catalogues, standardizing the continuous predictors and using group-clustered standard errors. The common adjustment set includes stellar mass, redshift, satellite/BGG rank, group luminosity and velocity dispersion when sufficiently complete.




For passive status, the CG$_4$ odds ratio is \(1.20\), with 95\% confidence interval \([0.88, 1.63]\) and \(p_{\rm Holm}=0.485\).




For passive satellite status, the CG$_4$ odds ratio is \(1.31\), with 95\% confidence interval \([0.95, 1.80]\) and \(p_{\rm Holm}=0.390\).




For star-forming satellite status, the CG$_4$ odds ratio is \(0.76\), with 95\% confidence interval \([0.56, 1.05]\) and \(p_{\rm Holm}=0.390\).




For elliptical morphology, the CG$_4$ odds ratio is \(1.62\), with 95\% confidence interval \([1.21, 2.16]\) and \(p_{\rm Holm}=0.007\).




For elliptical BGG morphology, the CG$_4$ odds ratio is \(0.90\), with 95\% confidence interval \([0.46, 1.74]\) and \(p_{\rm Holm}=0.751\).




For spiral morphology, the CG$_4$ odds ratio is \(0.62\), with 95\% confidence interval \([0.46, 0.83]\) and \(p_{\rm Holm}=0.007\).





Compact-group membership therefore remains independently associated with morphology after the available adjustments: CG$_4$ galaxies have a higher probability of being elliptical and a lower probability of being spiral. In contrast, the adjusted passive and star-forming satellite coefficients are weaker and do not provide corrected-significant evidence for an instantaneous star-formation difference.

Projected distance is retained as a phase-space and interaction diagnostic rather than matched away because it partly defines compactness and has limited overlap. The matched CG$_4$--minus--control difference in passive fraction is \(0.098\), with 95\% bootstrap interval \([0.017, 0.175]\) and \(p_{\rm Holm}=0.054\).

The strong-emission fraction is 32.3\% in CG$_4$, 41.0\% in Control4B, 45.7\% in Control4C, 56.9\% in RG4.


Relative to Control4B, the CG$_4$ strong-emission fraction differs by \(-0.087\) (\(p_{\rm Holm}=0.012\)).



Relative to Control4C, the CG$_4$ strong-emission fraction differs by \(-0.135\) (\(p_{\rm Holm}<10^{-3}\)).



Relative to RG4, the CG$_4$ strong-emission fraction differs by \(-0.246\) (\(p_{\rm Holm}<10^{-3}\)).


These limited H\(\alpha\) results indicate that CG$_4$ galaxies have a lower strong-emission fraction than each control sample in the current catalogue, but they do not provide a direct post-starburst or recently-quenched classification.

\section{Relation to recent structural studies of compact-group galaxies}

A complementary interpretation of the present results is provided by the recent series of studies by
\citet{Montaguth2023,Montaguth2025,Montaguth2026NonIsolated,Montaguth2026MassSize}.
Using multiwavelength S-PLUS imaging and single-S'ersic structural fits, these authors identified a population of
``transition’’ galaxies, broadly corresponding to red systems with relatively low or intermediate S'ersic index, whose
location in the ($R_{\rm e})-(n)$ plane suggests ongoing structural transformation in compact groups. In particular,
\citet{Montaguth2023} found that compact-group transition galaxies include a population that is smaller and more
centrally concentrated than analogous field galaxies, while \citet{Montaguth2025} showed that this signature is
stronger in non-isolated compact groups embedded in larger structures. \citet{Montaguth2026NonIsolated} further
compared non-isolated compact groups with their surrounding host-group galaxies and found that the compact-group
members display higher quenched fractions, a larger early-type fraction, and distinct transition-galaxy
($R_{\rm e})-(n)$ distributions. Finally, \citet{Montaguth2026MassSize} showed that the environmental dependence of the
stellar mass–size relation is weak for early- and late-type galaxies, but strongest for transition galaxies, whose
mass–size slopes and S'ersic-index distributions become more early-type-like in groups and compact groups.

Our analysis addresses a related but stricter question. Rather than comparing compact groups primarily with the
field, or non-isolated compact groups with their immediate host structures, we ask whether compact-group galaxies
remain different from galaxies in regular four-member group analogues after controlling for stellar mass, redshift,
rank, and available group-scale quantities. In this framework, the most robust residual signal is morphological:
compact-group membership remains associated with an enhanced probability of being classified as elliptical and a
reduced probability of being classified as spiral. By contrast, the passive and star-forming satellite fractions are
more sensitive to the adopted control sample and to the adjustment or matching procedure.

The two sets of results are therefore not contradictory. The structural work of
\citet{Montaguth2023,Montaguth2025,Montaguth2026NonIsolated,Montaguth2026MassSize} identifies the likely
nature of the transformation: disk fading, increasing central concentration, and/or bulge growth in galaxies occupying
an intermediate region between blue late-type disks and red early-type systems. Our results indicate that, when
compact groups are compared with regular-group analogues rather than with the field alone, the clearest surviving
signature is the accumulated morphological state of the galaxy population, whereas an instantaneous star-formation
offset is less robust. This suggests that compact groups should not be interpreted simply as environments with
ongoing enhanced quenching at the present epoch. They are more plausibly very dense regions in which past
interactions, local tidal density, and preprocessing in larger haloes have already shifted part of the galaxy population
toward earlier morphologies.

A useful way to reconcile these views is to regard the compact-group signal as primarily structural and cumulative.
In that picture, the compact configuration may accelerate morphological transformation, but some of the observed
differences may also reflect the fact that many compact groups are the dense cores or substructures of larger systems.
Future work combining the structural diagnostics of S-PLUS-like analyses, especially ($R_{\rm e}$), S\'ersic index,
transition-galaxy classifications, and mass–size residuals, with the matched regular-group control framework used
here would provide a direct test of whether the compact-group transformation is unique to compact configurations
or is the high-density limit of more general group preprocessing.

\section{Conclusion}

After removing systems classified as split, we find that compact groups differ from regular four-member groups mainly through their galaxy population mix. Galaxies in CG$_4$ are more frequently elliptical and less frequently spiral than galaxies in RG$_4$, with highly significant differences in both fractions. This morphological difference is also present among BGGs: CG$_4$ BGGs are more often elliptical than RG$_4$ BGGs.

The star-formation difference is more specific. CG$_4$ satellites are significantly less frequently star-forming than RG$_4$ satellites, while the star-forming fraction of BGGs does not differ significantly between the two samples. Moreover, among galaxies that remain star-forming, CG$_4$ galaxies do not show a significant offset from RG$_4$ galaxies relative to the star-forming main sequence.

The colour analysis does not indicate a simple uniform reddening of compact-group galaxies. The pooled mass- and redshift-adjusted comparison does not support a statistically robust blue CG$_4$ satellite offset. Negative coefficients appear after adding morphology and sSFR class, but the result is sensitive to the choice of control catalogue. Neither the sSFR-class nor morphology splits isolate a significant effect among star-forming spirals. The radial residual trends instead indicate that outer CG$_4$ satellites are bluer than inner satellites, consistent with stronger processing toward the BGG. The BGG and mean satellite colours are correlated, but the corresponding CG$_4$ relations do not differ significantly from those of the control catalogues. Likewise, the apparent colour difference between dominated and non-dominated BGGs disappears after controlling for stellar mass.

The most robust compact-group signal is morphological rather than an instantaneous star-formation offset. After controlling for stellar mass, redshift, rank and available group-scale properties, CG$_4$ membership remains associated with enhanced elliptical and reduced spiral probabilities, whereas passive status and satellite star-forming status do not remain significant in the corresponding adjusted models. The matched-control analysis supports the same morphological excess and gives only borderline evidence for an enhanced passive fraction.

The additional diagnostics do not favour a simple fossil-group or AGN-driven explanation. The magnitude-gap distributions of CG$_4$ and the pooled controls are statistically similar, and AGN-like activity is not independently enhanced after mass and environment adjustment. The limited H\(\alpha\) diagnostic shows a lower strong-emission fraction in CG$_4$ than in the control catalogues, but the absence of both \(D_n4000\) and H\(\delta_A\) prevents a direct post-starburst or recently-quenched classification.

Taken together, these results suggest that compact groups host a more morphologically evolved galaxy population, with an enhanced fraction of early-type galaxies and a reduced fraction of star-forming satellites. Because the passive CG$_4$ signal does not remain significant in both the adjusted and matched analyses, the apparent star-formation specialness is at least partly explained by environment, mass/rank mix, or pre-processing in dense halo cores. The morphology signal is more robust, but the projected tidal-index experiment suggests that local pairwise density may absorb part of that signal, so this result should be interpreted as evidence for accumulated transformation in very dense environments rather than as a uniquely isolated compact-group effect.


The selection audit does not remove all catalogue-systematic uncertainty, and the angular-neighbour diagnostic confirms the expected higher close-pair incidence in CG$_4$, but it does not identify a major availability imbalance under the adopted criterion.
